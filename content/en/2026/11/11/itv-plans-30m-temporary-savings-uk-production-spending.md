ITV plans to save £30m through what it calls "temporary" cuts, with £25m coming from content, according to The Guardian. The newspaper reports that the savings will be announced alongside new industry figures showing a sharp fall in spending on film and high-end television production in the UK.

The broadcaster, which has agreed to sell its television and streaming business to Sky, will find the other £5m elsewhere in the company. The Guardian understands that the programme cuts will mostly hit small external commissions, which will be pushed back into 2027. ITV indicated that the savings would not involve job cuts.

The Guardian places the plan against a difficult period for British TV, with commissions being dropped, advertising falling and jobs being cut across television and streaming. ITV expects advertising revenue to fall between 5% and 10% in the final quarter of the year, despite the usually busy Christmas period, and between 2% and 3% across the full year. Its chief executive, Carolyn McCall, blamed "continuing broader macroeconomic uncertainty" and growing digital advertising on global streaming platforms. She also pointed to a new rule this year that bans adverts for less healthy food before 9pm on TV. According to The Guardian, ITV shares fell almost 4% on Wednesday and have lost about a fifth of their value since January. The company is listed on the FTSE 250, an index of mid-sized companies on the London Stock Exchange.

The production figures come from the British Film Institute (BFI), the UK's film body. They cover films and high-end TV shows costing at least £1m an episode. The Guardian reports that spending on these productions by British and international broadcasters, Hollywood studios and streamers fell by £2.08bn year on year, to £5.2bn in the year to the end of September. The newspaper compares this with £7.26bn in the same period of 2025, a record year that included Avengers: Doomsday and the first series of the Harry Potter TV show. The Guardian gives the fall as 40%, but its own figures work out at roughly 28%, so this story uses the pound amounts.

The BFI's figures are provisional, labelled "first reported". The Guardian notes that the BFI usually revises spending upwards as more information arrives. The final 2025 figure, for example, was £900m higher than its first estimate. A BFI spokesperson described "a complex picture", ranging from shifts in the global industry to stronger competition from other countries, following a strong 2025. The BFI and the British Film Commission are monitoring the situation closely.

ITV's plans are also tied to its planned sale to Sky, which is owned by the US broadcaster Comcast. The Guardian reports that McCall sees the £1.6bn deal as ITV's route to survival. Last week, the culture secretary, Lisa Nandy, issued a public interest intervention notice, which triggers reviews by the media regulator, Ofcom, and the Competition and Markets Authority. Nandy's concerns include the combined group's share of about 86% of linear news and current affairs viewing in Britain, and possible US political influence over editorial independence. On Wednesday ITV said the deal was "progressing as planned" and still expected to complete in the second half of 2027.

ITV will keep its studios business, which makes programmes for ITV and other broadcasters and streamers. According to The Guardian, the company told investors that studio revenue fell 1% in the first nine months of the year. Even so, ITV produced five of the UK's ten most-watched dramas in the third quarter of 2026, including The Gentlemen for Netflix and The Blame for ITV.

Other British broadcasters are also cutting back. The Guardian reports that the BBC intends to shed as many as 2,000 jobs and that Channel 4 is planning layoffs.
