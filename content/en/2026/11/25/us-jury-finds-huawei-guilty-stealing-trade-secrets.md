A US jury has found Huawei Technologies guilty of conspiracy to steal trade secrets and of bank fraud. It also acquitted the Chinese telecommunications equipment maker of racketeering conspiracy, the most serious charge it faced.

The verdict was delivered on Tuesday in a federal court in Brooklyn, New York, after a trial of more than two months. Jurors convicted Huawei on 10 of the 12 counts it faced, which also included conspiracy to violate US sanctions. Huawei and three affiliates were charged in total.

The racketeering charge, which under US law is known as RICO, is normally used against organised crime and drug cases. Prosecutors used it to argue that Huawei had operated as a criminal enterprise in order to take intellectual property from US companies. Jurors declined to convict on that count, which required them to find that the company acted like a criminal organisation.

During the trial, prosecutors called more than 60 witnesses and said Huawei had spent around 20 years doing whatever it took to expand. They alleged that the company lied to banks about its business in Iran, traded in countries under US sanctions, including Iran and North Korea, and stole technology from rivals. Prosecutors also showed jurors video material and said that one Huawei employee had taken part of a testing robot belonging to T-Mobile, the US mobile operator. The defence called 15 witnesses.

Huawei's lawyer, Brian Heberlig, argued that prosecutors had decided in advance that the company was a criminal organisation and had built the case around isolated incidents. He said the firm had grown to operate in about 170 countries with more than 200,000 staff through hard work, innovation and investment in research and development. He also said much of the material the US alleged had been stolen was already publicly available.

In a statement, Huawei said the racketeering acquittal "confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years." The company added that it considered the remaining charges equally without legal or factual merit. Heberlig described the racketeering count as a "significant overreach" and said the company had mixed feelings about the rest of the verdict, since it had hoped for acquittals on every charge.

The case dates back to December 2018, when Meng Wanzhou, Huawei's chief financial officer and the daughter of its founder, was detained in Canada on a US warrant. The episode caused a diplomatic dispute between the US, China and Canada. Meng later entered a deferred prosecution agreement, returned to China and the charges against her were dropped. US authorities first announced charges against Huawei in 2019, the same year the company was placed on a trade blacklist that restricted its access to US technology. It has not been allowed to sell equipment to US mobile carriers for years because of security concerns.

Sentencing is planned for September 2027. Heberlig said the defence would push for the lightest sentence possible and that he expected Huawei to appeal. It is unclear what effect the verdict will have on the company's global business. Some experts have said the case has not undermined Huawei's operations, which have diversified as it has become a key player in China's push for artificial intelligence.
