Australia's parliament has passed a law that could expose the largest technology companies to fines running into the billions of dollars if they fail to keep users safe online. The Digital Duty of Care Act requires major platforms to reduce the risk of harm before it occurs, rather than responding once damage has been done, according to the Australian government.

The bill passed the House of Representatives, the lower house of parliament, by 97 votes to 43. The centre-left Labor government holds a majority there. Labor, the Greens and some crossbench MPs known as teals voted in favour. The opposition Coalition, led by the conservative Liberal Party, voted against, along with independent MPs Dai Le and Bob Katter and the One Nation party. Reports say the Coalition tried a last-minute procedural move to delay the bill, but it failed. The legislation has now passed both houses of parliament.

The Senate, the upper house, is the harder hurdle, because no party holds a majority there. The government struck a deal with the minor Greens party on Tuesday to get the bill through. Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young, who negotiated with Labor, is credited with significantly strengthening the text.

The law sets minimum safety standards for online services, including games, apps and artificial intelligence chatbots. Services must protect Australians from seriously harmful material and conduct. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has presented the measure as a way to protect children and society from online harm by placing responsibility on the platforms themselves.

The legislation is similar to the European Union's Digital Services Act, passed in 2022, and to Britain's Online Safety Act, passed a year later. Earlier this month, the European Commission also proposed a Digital Fairness Act, which would tackle manipulative online practices, improve price transparency and strengthen protections for children.

The opposition has strongly opposed the measure. Angus Taylor, the opposition leader, said the Liberal Party would repeal the law if it wins the 2028 election, and described it as authoritarian. "This bill is a blank check for political censorship," he said, arguing that it attacks free speech.

It is not yet clear from the reporting when the rules will take effect or how the fines would be calculated.
