A South African court has convicted a Zimbabwean man of planning to travel to Russia to fight in the war in Ukraine, in a case that highlights the recruitment of African citizens into the conflict. The defendant, who is 24, pleaded guilty and was sentenced on Tuesday, 24 November, to five years in prison. The sentence is suspended, so he will serve it only if he commits a similar offence within that period. He has also been ordered to be deported to Zimbabwe.

He was one of nine Zimbabweans arrested on 8 November in two linked operations. Five were detained at OR Tambo International Airport in Johannesburg, and four at a hotel in the city. South African police worked on the investigation with Interpol and other international law enforcement agencies. The four co-accused in the airport case have not yet entered pleas. They are due back at the Kempton Park Magistrates' Court on 1 December. The four arrested at the hotel are facing a separate court process.

In court, the defendant said he had applied online in Zimbabwe and agreed to fight for Russia in exchange for "remuneration". He said he had entered South Africa legally on 4 November and stayed in accommodation in Kempton Park while preparing to leave for Russia. Prosecutors have not publicly confirmed which side the group allegedly intended to fight for. Reports also give two different spellings of the defendant's surname, so this article does not use it.

South Africa's Foreign Military Assistance Act, passed in 1998, restricts unauthorised involvement in foreign conflicts. It was drafted when the government was concerned about former apartheid security personnel fighting elsewhere in Africa. The law prohibits mercenary activity and regulates recruitment and training for foreign armed forces, and it applies to foreign nationals as well as South Africans.

The case comes amid wider concern across Africa about citizens being recruited for the war. A Ukrainian intelligence assessment earlier this year estimated that more than 1,700 people from 36 African countries had been recruited to fight for Russia, and some have been captured and are held as prisoners of war. Zimbabwe's government said in March that 15 of its citizens had been killed and that more than 60 others were still on the frontlines. It said recruits had been promised well-paid jobs, had their travel documents taken and were forced into combat.

Kenya's figures have also varied. Its government said in February that more than 1,000 citizens had been recruited, and in March it estimated that 252 had been illegally conscripted. Kenya's foreign minister, Musalia Mudavadi, visited Moscow and said an agreement had been reached for Russia to stop recruiting Kenyans.

South Africa's government has condemned "the exploitation of young vulnerable people by individuals working with foreign military entities". Its authorities have also arrested people accused of recruiting others, including a journalist in December last year. The four remaining Zimbabweans in the airport case will return to court on 1 December.
