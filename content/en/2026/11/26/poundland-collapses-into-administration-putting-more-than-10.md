Poundland has collapsed into administration, a formal insolvency process in which an appointed administrator takes control of a company and seeks either to rescue it or sell its parts. The move puts more than 10,000 jobs at risk just weeks before Christmas. The discount chain, which has about 600 stores across the UK and Ireland, was placed into administration by its directors, and the administrators appointed are AlixPartners.

The figures differ depending on who is counting. AlixPartners said Poundland employs more than 10,000 people, and it is understood that is about 2,000 fewer than a month ago. One account puts the number of jobs at risk at 11,000. It has also been suggested that as many as 30,000 jobs in total could be affected once the supply chain and other businesses on high streets are included.

All stores and distribution centres remain open and continue to trade during the process. The administrators are considering all options, including selling the business and its assets in whole or in part. The business is expected to be broken up, with some stores sold to rival retailers such as Poundstretcher, B&M and The Range. Potentially hundreds of shops could be left empty.

The chain's US owner, Gordon Brothers, is a finance firm that specialises in buying and restructuring distressed companies. It bought Poundland for £1 last year from Pepco, the European retail group that previously owned it. Gordon Brothers says it lent the company £85m over a year to keep it solvent and stepped in when no one else was willing to finance the business. Critics, including MPs, have accused it of taking money out of the chain. Documents given to MPs showed close to £20m in interest payments and fees. Gordon Brothers has denied claims that it hoped to profit from breaking the company up.

A shareholder loan was one of the main obstacles in talks. Gordon Brothers was owed £22.5m of a £30m shareholder loan, and Pepco was owed the remaining £7.5m. Irish firm Martin Property Group offered to pay £5m up front and defer the rest to 2030. London-based Xyra offered £12.5m up front but ran out of time to finish a deal. Gordon Brothers rejected these offers, saying it could recover more from a break-up than from the solvent bids.

Poundland's managing director, Barry Williams, wrote to staff that the board had “no alternative but to take this decision”. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the news would be deeply concerning for workers and their families and that the government would work to secure a deal that protects jobs. Usdaw, the shopworkers' union, called it “devastating news for our members” and criticised Gordon Brothers for failing to engage with the union.

Liam Byrne, the Labour chair of the House of Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee, said the committee would recall Gordon Brothers to give evidence in public. Gordon Brothers said Poundland remained vulnerable to inflation and any fall in sales or margins, and that it had faced several years of instability.

No buyer for the whole business has been confirmed. Any sale or break-up is still to be decided, and the number of jobs that will ultimately be lost remains uncertain.
