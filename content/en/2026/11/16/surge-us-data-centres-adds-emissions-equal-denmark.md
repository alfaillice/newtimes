The rapid expansion of "hyperscale" data centres in the United States has produced planet-heating emissions roughly equal to what Denmark emits over a full year, according to new research published in the journal *Nature Sustainability*.

Hyperscale facilities are very large data centres, filled with computing equipment, that are used mainly to handle the growth of artificial intelligence (AI). The study examined 403 of them and estimated that, over the year to April 2025, they emitted about 25.7 million metric tonnes of carbon dioxide. Denmark, a Nordic country of about 6 million people, produces a similar amount of carbon pollution in a year. The researchers say the figure is two to three times higher than an earlier estimate of hyperscale emissions for 2018.

The same study estimates that these facilities consumed between 68 and 99 terawatt-hours of electricity, which it puts at about 1.8% of total US electricity use. More than half of the power they used came from fossil fuels, according to the research. Part of the growth is being met by new gas-fired power plants, some built on the same sites as the data centres, while solar and wind capacity that is already installed has been used less, according to reporting.

The study was led by Gianluca Guidi, with Francesca Dominici, a professor of biostatistics at Harvard University, as a co-author. Dominici said: "there has been an explosion in these datacenters". She added that because many facilities have opened since 2025, the figure should be seen as a lower bound. The study relied on federal government data. Gina McCarthy, a former climate adviser to President Joe Biden, described it as an "important step" towards assessing the impact of data centres.

Developers have argued that the energy and water used by their facilities is small compared with sectors such as agriculture. They also point to jobs and higher local tax revenue. Local opposition is nonetheless growing across the political spectrum. In the Memphis area of Tennessee, a cluster of data centres linked to Elon Musk's AI ambitions has faced protests and lawsuits over noise and air pollution. Reports say Musk's company has responded by buying homes of nearby residents.

A separate study, commissioned by the Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC), a US environmental group, looked at Stratos, a planned data centre in drought-hit Utah. It concluded that the site could cause health-related economic costs of up to $690m a year if it runs on the dirtiest fossil fuel option, releasing up to 3,140 tons of fine particulate matter. The developers have planned to use combined-cycle natural gas but have reportedly also considered renewables. Last month they filed a water application that reportedly said demand would be lower than past agricultural use on the site, a claim some critics have questioned.
