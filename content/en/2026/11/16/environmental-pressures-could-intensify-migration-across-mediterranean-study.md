The Union for the Mediterranean (UpM), a regional body, and the Mediterranean Experts on Climate and Environmental Change network (MedECC) have warned that worsening living conditions linked to climate change and scarce resources could drive new population movements in the region. The warning holds unless adaptation and mitigation are strengthened. The analysis was presented on Monday at the Antalya Climate Summit in Turkey by Piero Lionello, a climatologist at the University of Salento, and Joan Borrell, the UpM's deputy secretary general for stability and resilience.

The authors note that the Mediterranean is one of the world's busiest migration routes. More than 2.5 million people have crossed it towards Europe over the past four decades, and between 2014 and 2024 more than 30,000 people died or went missing on the way.

The report says that the role of environmental decline in migration depends largely on socioeconomic and geopolitical factors, but that the weight of climate change could grow, particularly in the most vulnerable areas with the least capacity to adapt. At present, environmental displacement tends to cover shorter distances, while socioeconomic and geopolitical factors weigh more heavily in cross-border movements. The study stresses that there is no single, direct link between environmental change and migration that applies to the whole Mediterranean. In its words, "the complexity of the issue, together with the lack of adequate data, makes empirical assessment difficult" (translated from Spanish).

According to the report, global warming affects key sectors such as agriculture, tourism and energy, slows development and adds to instability in a region already marked by large socioeconomic gaps. Countries in the south and east of the Mediterranean are particularly exposed. They face water scarcity, as well as the combined threat of rising sea levels and desertification.

The figures in the study are stark. By 2050, more than 20% of marine species of fishing interest in the Mediterranean could become extinct, and crop yields could fall by 17%. The authors warn that if the necessary policies are not adopted, the region's habitability could be "seriously affected", with average temperatures in 2100 up to 6°C above pre-industrial levels.

The report argues that policies prioritising development and regional cooperation, together with investment in green infrastructure, resilient agriculture and sustainable water management, can help prevent forced displacement. It also calls for more interdisciplinary research and better databases to understand environmental migration and anticipate how it will evolve.

Borrell said the Mediterranean faces the simultaneous pressures of climate change, geopolitical instability and migration, and that what affects one shore will affect the others in one way or another. He added that the region is not destined to be defined by a succession of crises, and that greater cooperation on climate action and sustainable development gives it more than enough capacity to turn its vulnerabilities into shared solutions.

Eda Acara, a scientist with MedECC, told Europa Press that migration policies should take into account the effects of climate change on migrants' health, especially agricultural workers, whose heat-related risks are often missing from official statistics. She said the report does not prescribe specific measures for governments, including on legal labour migration schemes. She also noted that most climate-related displacement happens within countries, and that funding for adaptation should be a global goal. She said developing countries at the COP31 climate talks are calling for adaptation finance to be tripled, and that there is resistance to increasing it.

On cooperation between Spain and North African countries over water stress, Acara said they should strengthen financial and technological ties, adopt local and regional adaptation measures and share innovations, especially in irrigation. In her words, "there should be more innovation between countries that share the same problems" (translated from Spanish).
