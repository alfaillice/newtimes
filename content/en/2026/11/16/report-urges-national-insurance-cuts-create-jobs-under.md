An independent report by former Labour minister Alan Milburn will urge the government to lower the employer National Insurance (NI) contributions firms pay on younger workers, in order to create more jobs for people under 25, the BBC reports. The review, which has been in preparation for a year, is due to be published in full on Tuesday.

The BBC says there are nearly one million 16 to 24-year-olds in the UK who are not in employment, education or training, known as Neets, according to official figures. The report is expected to suggest starting the reduction with retail and hospitality firms, which the BBC says provide almost 40% of jobs for these young people.

Under current rules, the BBC explains, employers pay no NI for workers under 21 or for apprentices under 25, unless their earnings exceed £50,270 a year. Non-apprentices aged 21 to 24 pay 15% on annual earnings above £5,000. That system has applied since April 2025, when the then Chancellor Rachel Reeves raised the rate from 13.8% and lowered the threshold from £9,100.

According to the BBC, the report's proposals include doubling the threshold for 21 to 24-year-olds from £5,000 to £10,000, and stopping under-25s from claiming the health element of Universal Credit and personal independence payment (Pip), the main disability benefit in England and Wales. It would also introduce a legal guarantee of a job, training or employment support, with young people who refuse it potentially losing their benefits.

The BBC reports that a hairdresser who owns three salons in Nottingham, Collette Osborne, said rising employment costs had forced her to stop taking on apprentices. She said she used to employ eight a year but can no longer afford any, and that her workforce has fallen from more than double its current size to 21 staff. She estimated that training an apprentice costs around £60,000 before they can work with customers. "I feel suffocated, suffocated by employment costs," she told the BBC.

Milburn is expected to argue that an immediate, across-the-board cut in employer NI would not be the best use of several billions of pounds of public money, the BBC says. It cites research by the Resolution Foundation, a left-leaning think tank, which estimates that scrapping NI immediately for 21 to 24-year-olds would cost £5.1bn. Milburn is also reported to say the increase in NI for firms did not cause the Neets problem, and to support the government subsidising wages to encourage businesses to hire young workers. He told the BBC's Laura Kuenssberg programme that many young people arrive at work with mental health problems, ADHD or anxiety, which small businesses have to absorb as a cost.

The opposition's shadow work and pensions secretary, Helen Whately, said the government should "just stop doing the taxing", according to the BBC.

On Sunday, Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden said the government would extend its subsidised jobs guarantee to 18 to 24-year-olds claiming Universal Credit. Those who have been on benefits for 12 months will now be eligible, down from 18 months. The scheme, which guarantees a six-month job, will be available in England, Scotland and Wales. McFadden said it would help more than 90,000 young people into work by March 2029, the BBC reports.

The BBC's account does not say whether the government will adopt Milburn's recommendations. The full report is due on Tuesday.
