Australia's largest medical research organisation has advised that puberty blockers can be appropriate for gender-affirming care of young people in certain circumstances, according to The Guardian. The advice follows a year-long review of international evidence by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), an independent statutory agency funded by the Commonwealth government. The federal government had asked the council to develop it last year, in response to bans on the drugs in public hospitals in Queensland and the Northern Territory, which their state and territory governments had imposed.

The Guardian reports that the New Zealand government later introduced a similar restriction, despite widespread opposition across the health sector and warnings that such bans would harm children. The newspaper describes the NHMRC's position as at odds with those bans.

The council's interim national clinical practice guidelines, released on Monday, advise conditional use of puberty suppressants for children. According to The Guardian, the advice could put pressure on state and territory governments to reconsider their positions.

Puberty blockers are drugs that stop the body producing oestrogen and testosterone. They have been used since the 1970s to treat conditions such as precocious puberty, and in gender-affirming care since the 1990s. The guidelines describe gender incongruence as the clinical term for a difference between a person's gender and the sex recorded at birth. Some young people with gender incongruence also experience significant distress, known as gender dysphoria.

The council also rejected alternative approaches proposed by opponents of gender-affirming care, such as a 'wait and see' model. It said withholding care from children "can affect the young person's physical and mental health, wellbeing, and ability to function".

The guidelines do not require a child to be distressed before receiving treatment for gender incongruence. They recommend that decisions about care by a multidisciplinary team be made on a case-by-case basis, and that doctors prioritise the best interest and wellbeing of the young person while respecting their autonomy. The Guardian reports that the advice largely aligns with the AusPATH Australian Standards of Care, which it describes as Australia's only existing guidelines for doctors.

The interim advice, titled Advice on the use of puberty suppressants in the care of trans and gender-diverse people aged under 18, is open to public consultation until 18 January. The full guidelines, which will also cover gender-affirming hormones, are expected to be published in 2028.

The account in this article is based on reporting by The Guardian. The NHMRC's advice itself has not been checked against the council's published document for this story.
