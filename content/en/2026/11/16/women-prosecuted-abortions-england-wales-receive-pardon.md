The measure follows votes in Parliament. A House of Lords vote earlier this year introduced legislation to pardon these women and expunge their records. The House of Commons had already voted to change the abortion laws so that women cannot be criminalised for ending their own pregnancies in future, while keeping the existing legal framework for terminations. Many of the earlier cases were brought under Victorian legislation dating back to 1861, the paper notes.

Packer also said the records had made it difficult for some women to get and keep jobs. Many of the women had no other convictions or cautions, but arrests shown on enhanced Disclosure and Barring Service checks meant some had to disclose traumatic medical events to employers, even though they were never charged with a crime.

Not all campaigners consider the pardon sufficient. According to the paper, some women are grateful but want a formal apology from the government. One woman said the pardon did not undo years of stigma, and that publicity around her case meant people in her community still knew who she was and where she lived.

Dr Alison Wright, president of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), said the pardons recognised the injustice of historical convictions. Heidi Stewart, chief executive of the British Pregnancy Advisory Service (BPAS), said the change recognised the profound harm caused by criminalisation and was a meaningful step towards justice.
