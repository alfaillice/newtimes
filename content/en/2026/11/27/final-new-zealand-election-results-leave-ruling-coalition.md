Final results from New Zealand's general election show that the ruling coalition is one seat short of the 66 needed for a majority in the 130-seat Parliament. It will need the support of a minor party to form a government, or the country may have to return to the polls.

The count was released on Friday by the Electoral Commission, the body that runs New Zealand's elections, after nearly 485,000 "special votes" were counted. These are ballots cast from overseas or outside the voter's own electorate, and in previous elections they have tended to favour the left.

The National Party, led by Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, dropped two seats to 40. Its coalition partners are the libertarian ACT party, with 13 seats, and the populist New Zealand First, with 12. Together they hold 65 seats. Labour, the centre-left opposition led by Chris Hipkins, stayed on 36, while the Greens gained one seat to reach 17. Te Pāti Māori, which represents Māori interests, has five seats. The new centrist Opportunity party, led by Auckland businesswoman Qiulae Wong, won seven seats and is now the kingmaker.

Electorate counts changed three seats after the special votes were added. Labour took Wairarapa from National by 81 votes and Hamilton East by 173 votes, while the Greens won Wellington Bays from Labour with 1,610 votes. Had National won back just one of those seats through a recount, it could govern with a slim majority without Opportunity. Applications for a recount must be made by 3 December, and the process could take another five days.

Luxon had earlier ruled out working with Opportunity but has since softened his stance. He said National would begin formal talks with New Zealand First, ACT and Opportunity, and warned that the discussions would be "challenging" and could take some time. "Kiwis won't thank us for rushing to an agreement that falls apart or for sending them back to the polls," he said.

Wong confirmed that she would speak with Luxon. Her party has said it will negotiate with the party that wins the most seats. She said Opportunity's bottom lines, which include action on the supermarket duopoly, capping political donations, a ten-year cross-party health plan, ending bottom trawling and stronger climate commitments, were non-negotiable. She also said she would not make a deal if New Zealand First leader Winston Peters became foreign minister, a post he has held three times. New Zealand First and ACT have sharply different climate policies, and ACT is unlikely to back Opportunity's push for donation reform, which could complicate the talks.

Hipkins said Labour was proud to have won the highest share of the party vote but was disappointed it could not deliver a change of government. "More New Zealanders voted for change at this election than for the status quo," he said. It is his second election defeat, and he said a decision on his leadership would come after a government has been formed.

Negotiations could be lengthy. In 2023 talks took five weeks. With Christmas four weeks away, New Zealand may have to wait until 2027 to know the shape of its next government.
