Hospitals in England are preparing for a steep rise in flu cases. Admissions for the virus are at their highest for this time of year since before the Covid-19 pandemic, according to the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), the government body responsible for public health protection.

Sir Jim Mackey, the outgoing chief executive of NHS England, the body that oversees the publicly funded National Health Service (NHS) in England, said hospital bosses should consider asking staff and visitors to wear masks. He spoke at an NHS Alliance conference in London. Mackey said more hospital beds were occupied by flu patients than at the same point last year, and he expects the rise to build over the next five to 10 days. He described it as a “horrible spike”.

The UKHSA said the admission rate for flu rose sharply in the most recent week, from about five per 100,000 people to almost nine per 100,000, an increase of almost 80%. It is the highest rate for this point in November in this decade. Flu is still circulating at “moderate” levels, while Covid and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) have also risen but remain at low levels.

Several NHS trusts, the local bodies that run hospitals and services, have been under pressure this week. Mackey also criticised “corridor care”, in which patients wait, often unsupervised, on chairs and trolleys in corridors near emergency departments, known as A&E units. He said the practice had become normalised in too many places and was embedded in parts of the health service, raising concerns about both patient safety and dignity.

Vaccination rates are a concern. The UKHSA said uptake among people aged 65 and over was just under 68%, which is lower than usual for this time of year. Of the roughly 9.5 million people under 65 in clinical risk groups, including those with lung conditions, kidney or liver disease and diabetes, only 34% had received a flu vaccine by 22 November. The agency said this left almost 7 million people without protection. Among toddlers aged two and three, 37% of those eligible had taken up the nasal spray vaccine, which is free at pharmacies. School-age children are usually vaccinated at school, and catch-up sessions in pharmacies run until 1 December.

Dr Suzanna McDonald, the UKHSA’s flu lead, urged eligible people to come forward. “Please don’t dismiss the importance of a flu vaccine for your children,” she said. She added that thousands of people die from flu each year and that flu levels are expected to rise further as Christmas approaches and people socialise indoors. She also advised anyone who is ill with respiratory symptoms to wash their hands with warm soapy water, cough or sneeze into their elbow or a tissue, and stay at home.

Sir Ciaran Devane, chief executive of the NHS Alliance, which represents employers, said the flu season is arriving earlier than usual and could add to pressure on services heading into another difficult winter. He noted that the NHS had already faced heatwaves this summer. Officials say they do not yet know when the flu peak will arrive. Last year an early rise also raised concern, but the outbreak peaked early and the worst predictions did not materialise. This year admissions are slightly higher than a year ago. Staff are also dealing with a rise in patient numbers across a range of illnesses, which is partly linked to an ageing population.
