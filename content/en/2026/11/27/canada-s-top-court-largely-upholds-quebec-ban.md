Canada's Supreme Court has largely upheld Quebec's secularism law, leaving in place a ban on public sector workers wearing religious symbols at work. The ruling, issued on Friday 27 November, settles a long legal challenge to the measure, known as Bill 21, which the provincial government passed in 2019.

The law bars people in certain public jobs from wearing visible religious symbols such as hijabs, turbans, kippahs and crosses while working. Those covered include public school teachers, police officers, lawyers and judges. Teachers already in their posts when the law took effect could keep wearing symbols as long as they stayed in the same role with the same employer. Quebec has since extended the restrictions to school support staff, volunteers and daycare workers.

The case turned on the “notwithstanding clause”, a provision of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. It allows federal and provincial governments to keep a law in force even if it breaches certain Charter rights, including freedom of religion and equality. An override lasts up to five years and can be renewed. Quebec invoked the clause when it passed Bill 21, before any court had ruled on the law, and renewed it in 2024.

Chief Justice Richard Wagner, writing for three justices, said Bill 21 was rooted in Quebec’s “legitimate and historical commitment” to a secular state. He concluded that the province had validly used its power to keep the law in force. The court made one exception. It found that barring elected members of the National Assembly, Quebec’s provincial parliament, from taking part in the legislative process because they wear face coverings for religious reasons infringes democratic rights.

The court also addressed the wider use of the clause. It said politicians can invoke it without waiting for a court to rule on whether a law breaches the Charter. By four votes to three, it also endorsed the power of lower courts to declare that a law violates Charter rights even when the clause keeps it in effect. It said it would not make such a declaration in this case.

Justices Andromache Karakatsanis and Michelle O’Bonsawin dissented in part. They wrote that there was no question the law violates freedom of religion, and described Muslim women who had been turned away from jobs because they wear a hijab. They said the law had also prevented some people from taking promotions or transfers because they would lose their exemptions. “The Act’s effects are not experienced equally,” they wrote. “They fall most heavily on Muslim women.” Justice Nicholas Kasirer was also named among the justices who wrote that the clause cannot be used to authorise laws incompatible with Canada as a free and democratic country.

Civil liberty groups in Quebec have criticised the law, arguing that it disproportionately affects Muslim women and has excluded them from key areas of public life. Quebec’s government has defended the measure as constitutionally valid. The province’s premier, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, leader of the governing Parti Québécois, had said before the ruling that Quebec’s right to decide its own laws was at risk. The decision was welcomed in Quebec, where the governing party has clashed with the federal government and with national institutions such as the Supreme Court.

The ruling comes on the same day a separate appeal is due to be heard over a lower court decision requiring Quebec’s members of the National Assembly to swear allegiance to King Charles before sitting in the chamber. Some lawmakers from several parties in the province have refused to take the oath.
