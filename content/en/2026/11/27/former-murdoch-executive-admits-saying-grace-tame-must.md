Matt Handbury, a former executive at media businesses owned by his uncle Rupert Murdoch, has admitted saying that Grace Tame “must be looking to be raped again”. Tame, a survivor of child sexual abuse who was named Australian of the Year in 2021 for her advocacy work, says she overheard the remark at a function last week and confronted him.

Tame described the incident in an opinion piece published on Friday. She wrote that she heard Handbury make the comment to another man at a University of New South Wales event, where former military lawyer David McBride received an award. She did not realise who he was until a bystander told her. When she confronted him, he introduced himself as “Matt”. She went back to him later in the evening to check that she had heard him correctly.

Handbury, who is 77, apologised by email the following day. The message, which journalists have seen, says he had made reference to Tame’s “strikingly attractive get-up” and then repeated the remark. He described it as being in “poor taste” and, more strongly, “highly objectionable”. He said he regretted it as soon as he left the room, thanked Tame for “calling me out”, and added that no one could deny she had looked “absolutely fabulous” that night.

In further correspondence with Australian media, Handbury said he did not recall using the phrase “tits hanging out”, which Tame attributed to him in her piece. He said the words he remembered were closer to asking whether she had seen Grace Tame and remarking on her outfit, followed by the reference to rape.

Tame was 15 when she was groomed and sexually abused by her maths teacher, who was later convicted and jailed. In her piece, she said the remark was especially painful because it referred to a real, tested trauma rather than an imagined harm. “It swiftly reawakened deep-seated physical wounds and haunting memories. How is that funny?” she wrote. Speaking to Australian media, she said the comment had triggered deep trauma and left her feeling destabilised, because her body was in survival mode.

Tame wrote that she was struck by the fact that, in a room of activists, journalists and lawyers, nobody other than her challenged Handbury at the time. She thanked the bystanders who supported her afterwards and said she was tired of being provoked and then asked why she was angry. She also linked the incident to a wider culture of normalised violence and self-censorship, citing research suggesting that misogynistic attitudes are a broad predictor of violent extremism.

Karen Bevan, chief executive of Full Stop Australia, said that remarks like this are repeated in workplaces, pubs and group chats, where nobody records them or writes about them afterwards, and urged people to speak up when they hear them.

At the time of writing, the sources do not report any public response from Handbury beyond the email and his comments in further correspondence. He had not responded to requests for comment from journalists, and a request to a rehabilitation centre linked to him was also made.
