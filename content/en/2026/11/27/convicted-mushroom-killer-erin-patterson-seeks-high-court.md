Erin Patterson, the Australian woman convicted of murdering three relatives with poisoned food, has applied to the High Court of Australia for permission to challenge her convictions. The application was filed on Friday, the last day of a 28-day window that opened when the Court of Appeal of Victoria, the state where the crime took place, issued its judgment last month. It is the final legal route open to her in the case.

Patterson, 52, hosted a lunch at her home in rural Victoria in 2023. She served beef Wellington containing death cap mushrooms to her estranged husband's parents, Don and Gail Patterson, and to Gail's sister, Heather Wilkinson. All three died. Heather's husband, Ian Wilkinson, a local pastor, also ate the meal. He survived after spending weeks in hospital and still has health problems linked to the poisoning. Simon Patterson, Erin Patterson's estranged husband, had been due to attend but cancelled at the last minute, partly because he believed his wife had repeatedly tried to poison him over several years.

A jury found Patterson guilty of the three murders and of the attempted murder of Ian Wilkinson after a high-profile trial last year. She was sentenced last September to life imprisonment with a non-parole period of 33 years. The trial judge set that period because of the harsh prison conditions she would likely face.

In October, three judges of the Court of Appeal, Lesley Taylor, Stephen McLeish and Peter Kidd, rejected her appeal against her convictions. They also upheld an appeal by the Director of Public Prosecutions, the official who leads criminal prosecutions in Victoria, against the sentence. They found the original term too lenient and ordered that she be resentenced to life without parole. In their judgment of 30 October, they said the offending was "extraordinarily cold and calculated" and had to be denounced in the strongest terms.

Her lawyers had argued that her convictions should be overturned on seven grounds. The court refused leave to appeal on most of them. Two grounds were allowed to be heard but were also dismissed. One concerned the jury staying at the same motel as the police informant and staff from the Director of Public Prosecutions while they deliberated. The other concerned mobile phone cell tower evidence and sightings recorded on the citizen science website iNaturalist, which placed her near towns where death cap mushrooms had been reported.

The High Court is Australia's top court and does not have to take up a case. It hears appeals in only about 10 per cent of the cases put to it. Her written application will be considered by all seven justices, who may decide it on paper or refer it to an oral hearing. The court's last sitting period of the year runs from 30 November to 11 December, and decisions are usually published the following Thursday. If the application is not decided by then, the court does not sit again until 1 February. The court has declined to comment on the application.
