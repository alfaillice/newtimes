Alan Milburn, a former Labour health secretary, has proposed sweeping reforms that he says could help 440,000 more young people into work, the BBC reports. His government-commissioned review is due to be published in full on Tuesday, and the broadcaster has set out its main recommendations ahead of publication.

According to the BBC, almost one million people aged 16 to 24 in the UK are not in education, employment or training, a group known as Neets. The current rate is 13%, and Milburn wants it to fall to around 5% within a decade. He describes the situation as a "lost generation" in the making and as a "moral" crisis.

The review calls for government action from early childhood through to adulthood. It says that in 2024/25, for every £1 spent helping young people into work or education, £25 was spent on youth benefits. By 2036, it wants the ratio to be £2 on benefits for every £1 on work support. Among its targets is a new aim for 90% of children to be school-ready with basic life skills by the age of five. The BBC notes that the figure is 68% in England, and that the government's existing target is 75% by 2028.

At the centre of the plan is a legal entitlement to support, delivered through a new "Youth Opportunity Service" in England. Under it, a young person would be linked with a named adviser and helped to find an apprenticeship, a college place, supported employment or a job within four months.

To pay for this, the BBC reports that young adults would no longer qualify for the health top-up of Universal Credit or for Personal Independence Payment (Pip). They would instead receive a Youth Allowance at the base rate of Universal Credit for young people, plus a new Additional Cost Allowance to replace Pip. A small group who could never work would be covered by a new Severe and Complex Needs Insurance. The BBC profiled Kat, 21, from Devon, who has fluctuating health problems and may receive much less under the new system.

Other measures include gradually lowering the employer National Insurance rate for young workers. The existing £3,000 Youth Jobs Grant and the Jobs Guarantee would be kept. The BBC also reports that the government has recently said young people will become eligible for the guarantee after 12 months on Universal Credit, rather than 18.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies, according to the BBC, has said the reforms are more likely to work if support for young people is balanced with incentives for businesses to hire them. It warned that cutting benefits would reduce young people's income straight away, and that higher employment could take longer to make up the difference for some.

Political reaction was mixed. The BBC reports that Helen Whately, the Conservative shadow work and pensions secretary, said some proposals were "too cautious" and relied on the "expensive hand of the state". She said the review "fails to bring down the welfare bill". The Liberal Democrats' Daisy Cooper said cutting National Insurance for young workers would only partly undo the damage of Labour's job tax. Reform UK's Suella Braverman said waiting ten years for change would be "consigning another generation to the scrap heap". The Greens' deputy leader, Mothin Ali, called the proposals announced so far "extremely concerning".
