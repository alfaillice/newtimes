Actors in the UK are to gain more control over their “digital replicas” under a new agreement on artificial intelligence protections with television and film producers, according to The Guardian. The deal was secured by Equity, the actors’ union, after a growing backlash on set against the use of AI and body scans.

The three-year agreement gives performers the right to be paid when their digital replicas are used, and to consent to whether a replica is used at all, the newspaper reports. If a digital double or digital alteration is used to make “substantive changes” to scenes involving agreed levels of nudity or simulated sexual activity, producers must obtain the actor’s approval. The Guardian also reports that producers may not use a digital replica during a strike where consent is required for that use.

Contracts will be drafted so that producers obtain permission for a specific project, rather than the “blanket consent” that some producers had sought over an actor’s replica. The deal was negotiated over several years with the Producers Alliance for Cinema and Television (Pact), the body that represents production companies. According to the paper, it is an attempt to set ground rules for the use of an actor’s image as AI develops rapidly.

The agreement includes a 24% rise in pay rates for major films and a 9% rise for TV projects. Its leadership recommends that the union’s 8,000 members approve it, and they will vote on it. The Guardian notes that in an indicative ballot last December, Equity members voted 99% in favour of industrial action and of refusing to be scanned on set without further AI protections.

The Guardian reports that Equity acknowledged some areas the deal could not cover, such as the digital scanning traditionally carried out so that effects can be added later. The union said that, despite its efforts, the agreement does not set explicit contract protections against using actors’ performances to train AI systems. Equity also told members that digital replicas can be made from the footage shot for the production itself, without scanning the performers.

Concern about scanning has grown on set. Many performers fear that scan data could be used to train AI models on their likenesses or poses, which could in turn reduce their work. The newspaper recalled that last year the actor Olivia Williams told The Guardian that actors should have as much control over data taken from scans of their bodies as they have over nudity scenes. She said performers were regularly asked to be scanned by banks of cameras, with few guarantees about how the data would be used.

The deal also includes other provisions, according to the paper. Producers must limit access to digital replicas to people with a legitimate business need and make reasonable efforts to protect them from unauthorised use or copying. It also commits to safe casting and audition spaces, training and reporting on sexual harassment, and adequate hair and makeup provision for artists of all ethnicities and cultures.

Paul W Fleming, Equity’s general secretary, said: “We’ve achieved the best AI provisions in the business – protecting performers and ensuring that human performance is valued and prioritised across the industry.”
