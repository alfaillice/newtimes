The Trump administration has tightened its rules on advanced computer chips, targeting routes that Beijing has used to reach American technology. On Monday, Washington introduced interim rules that require a US licence before advanced chips can be shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. The US suspects these countries of acting as conduits for chips reaching China. The timing is sensitive: President Donald Trump is due to meet Chinese leader Xi Jinping on Thursday on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen.

The rules go beyond physical shipments. Under the new measures, any data centre in the world that uses Nvidia chips would need Washington's permission before renting computing power to Chinese customers. Data centres are large facilities full of servers that run artificial intelligence (AI) systems, and renting their capacity has been a way for Chinese firms to access advanced processing power without buying chips outright. The US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security said the aim is to stop sensitive technology being circumvented through remote computing in third countries. Licences will be decided case by case.

The impact on South East Asia is uncertain. Analysts quoted in reporting said Malaysia and Thailand, where data centre growth has been driven heavily by Chinese buyers, could be hit hardest. Another analyst, from Verisk Maplecroft, argued that the wider data centre boom is unlikely to be derailed, particularly investment backed by large tech firms with clearly identified customers. Singapore said it is engaging US authorities to understand the rules and is exploring how to facilitate trusted access for local firms. Brazil's Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services said it is monitoring the situation.

China's foreign ministry described the restrictions as economic coercion and said Beijing would take necessary measures to protect its interests. The Institute for Progress, a US think-tank, projects that at least 30 per cent of China's advanced AI computing power will come from renting overseas or from smuggled chips in 2027, according to reporting. Some analysts believe tighter limits could push Chinese firms towards domestic chips.

The policy has also exposed a rift inside Washington. According to reporting, the rules were written by the Treasury Department and the White House rather than by the Commerce Department, which normally oversees chip exports. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has pressed for stricter enforcement of existing rules. "We have all the authorities that we need. They just need to be implemented," he said on Friday. Bessent also said four government agencies, including the Treasury, had pushed for months to put Megaspeed, a Singapore data centre firm, on a US trade blacklist, and that Commerce had not acted. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has been a vocal supporter of allowing Nvidia to sell chips to China. His department has been accused of ignoring chip smuggling and delaying a planned crackdown, a charge that has been reported.

Megaspeed, which bought more than $4.8bn of Nvidia chips in about two years, denies that it smuggled chips to China and says its business is legitimate. US authorities suspect that many of those chips were diverted. Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed director whom some US officials suspect of working for Chinese intelligence, is linked to the affair that led Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, to resign last week. China denies the spying claim.

The Republican chair of the House Select Committee on China, John Moolenaar, welcomed the new restrictions. "Our most advanced AI chips are simply not for sale to our foremost enemy," he said. Lutnick and Jensen Huang have been summoned to testify before the Senate on December 3.
