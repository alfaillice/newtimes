Hundreds of survivors of clergy sexual abuse have started receiving money from a $305m settlement linked to the Catholic archdiocese of New Orleans, according to The Guardian. The newspaper reported that more than half of the fund has now been paid out, a step that it describes as the beginning of the end of a legal dispute that has run for years in the southern US state of Louisiana.

The archdiocese filed for federal bankruptcy protection in May 2020. The Guardian reports that Don Massey, the trustee overseeing the settlement fund, confirmed on Friday that $180m had been distributed to 450 of about 740 survivors. Payments went either directly to survivors or through their lawyers where they had legal representation. Massey said he and his staff were working to get money to the remaining survivors by the end of the year, but they were still waiting for hundreds of distribution forms from survivors and their attorneys.

Some survivors who had been paid by Friday expressed a mix of relief, appreciation and sadness. One said the payment was ‘a weight off my shoulders’ after such a long period. Others told the newspaper they felt the process had been drawn out by being forced into bankruptcy court, rather than reaching similar settlements outside it, as some other large archdioceses in the US had done.

The payments took longer than church leaders and attorneys had promised at the confirmation hearings in December 2025, the Guardian reports. The archdiocese paid the full $305m into the trust soon after those hearings. However, a points-based system to set the amount of each payment, intended to reflect the severity and impact of each case, took months to put in place over the summer of 2026. Massey also told a court that the church had been slow to hand over the records needed to calculate payments.

Another source of delay was a dispute with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), the US federal agency that oversees Medicare and Medicaid, the government health programmes. Some payments were subject to liens that could claw back part of a survivor’s settlement if they had received those benefits. The CMS argued that the money should repay health costs linked to the trauma, such as mental health care. According to Massey, he persuaded the agency that the settlement payments were not subject to those clawbacks. He said about 30 Medicare liens were still being worked on.

The New Orleans archdiocese is the second-oldest Catholic diocese in the US. It is one of more than 40 Catholic institutions in the country that have sought bankruptcy protection amid the financial fallout of the church’s clergy abuse scandal. The Guardian, citing information from the Penn State University law school, reports that 29 of those cases had been settled as of Friday.

When the archdiocese filed, then archbishop Gregory Aymond estimated that settling the case would cost less than $7m, including compensation for victims. The final cost was far higher. Louisiana’s legislature opened a window allowing people to seek civil damages for childhood sexual abuse regardless of when it occurred, and the Guardian reports that legal and professional fees alone rose above $50m. In 2022 the case became especially contentious when a judge fined a survivors’ attorney $400,000 for breaching an order meant to keep certain information confidential. The attorney’s appeal, which reached the US Supreme Court, was unsuccessful.

Aymond retired in February, and his successor, archbishop James Checchio, reopened an internal investigation of all clergy accused of abuse in the bankruptcy. The Guardian reports that this process led to a former high school chaplain, who has since died, being added to the church’s list of clergy considered credibly accused of abuse.
