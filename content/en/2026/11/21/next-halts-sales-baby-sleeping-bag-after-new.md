Next, the British fashion retail chain, has halted sales of one of its own-brand baby sleeping bags and started a product recall, after a new mother raised concerns about its safety, the BBC reports.

Emma Hunter, 29, from Norwich, contacted BBC Your Voice to say that the neck opening of the bag was far too big. She said this could allow her daughter Rosie to slide down inside it, cover her face and risk suffocation. "I woke up my husband and we both agreed there was no way we could put Rosie in it," she told the BBC.

According to the BBC, the product is a pink floral 2.5 tog sleep bag with removable sleeves, sold in three sizes. Next said it had been removed from its website and all of its stores, and that a recall was in place. The company said it was contacting customers who had bought the item directly to tell them the appropriate next steps.

Emma told the BBC that she began checking the product the same night she noticed the problem. The bag carried a label saying it met British standards, but she found that the maximum neck circumference for that size should be 32cm. When she measured it, the opening was 36cm. Rosie measured 59cm when the bag was bought.

The BBC reports that Emma raised the issue with Next and that the company first offered her a refund. She told the broadcaster she asked whether the item should still be on sale and whether it should be recalled. Next replied that it was looking into the matter and that its product team was examining the bag.

The BBC also cites the British Retail Consortium (BRC), which says a significant hazard arises when a child slips through the neck opening of a sleep bag and the material covers the nose and mouth. The BRC warns that this can lead to oxygen deprivation, brain damage or death. The EN 16781 standard sets minimum and maximum neck sizes in relation to a child's height. For a child between 50cm and 65cm tall, the BBC says, the neck circumference should be between 28cm and 32cm.

Ruth Watts, a qualified health visitor in England, told the BBC that babies vary in size even at the same age. She said a sleeping bag should not be used if it covers the chin or face, has a hood or has no arms. She advised parents to check the gap around the neck: if one or two fingers fit, it is safe, and if it is tighter or looser, the bag should not be used.

The Lullaby Trust, a UK charity that works to prevent sudden, unexpected infant deaths, including Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS), told the BBC that it cannot comment on the safety of specific products because it does not carry out independent product safety testing. Its chief executive, Jenny Ward, said families should check that products have passed the necessary safety checks and meet British safety standards, and should stop using any product they are worried about. She said the charity supported a recent warning from the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS) about certain baby sleep bags. Emma's older brother died of SIDS as a baby, and she told the BBC that this is why the issue matters to her.

People can check whether a product has been recalled on the OPSS website, the BBC notes.
