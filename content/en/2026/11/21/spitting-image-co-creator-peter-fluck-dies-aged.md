Peter Fluck, who co-created the British satirical puppet series *Spitting Image*, has died aged 85, the BBC reports. According to the broadcaster, his family said he died in the early hours of Saturday at home in Cornwall, following a recent illness.

The show ran from 1984 to 1996 across 18 seasons on ITV and used puppet caricatures of celebrities, politicians and members of the royal family. Its targets included former Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher and the Queen Mother. The BBC describes the programme as immensely popular and sometimes controversial. It won several awards, including one Bafta TV award and two Emmys, and at its peak drew an audience of 15 million, the broadcaster says.

Fluck was born in Cambridge in 1941. He created the show with his childhood friend Roger Law, whom he had met while studying at the Cambridge School of Art. After leaving school, the two moved to London to work as freelance illustrators. In 1976 they set up their partnership, “Luck and Flaw”, back in Cambridge.

The BBC quotes an interview Fluck gave to ArtCornwall in 2006, in which he said: “You could say we took the traditional newspaper editorial cartoon and put it on the screen.” He described the show as being broadcast at “the difficult time of 10 o’clock on a Sunday night when all politicians should have been in bed”. Many characters parodied Thatcher’s cabinet. Douglas Hurd was shown with “Mr Whippy ice cream” hair, and her successor, John Major, was portrayed as a dull, grey puppet with a fondness for peas. Fluck said in a 2011 interview that many politicians had asked for their puppets to be made, because “if you didn’t appear as a puppet on Spitting Image you hadn’t made it as a politician”.

ITV eventually axed the series because of declining audience numbers. Fluck then moved to Cornwall, where he turned to ceramics, printmaking and mobiles. In 2018, Law donated his archive of the show to Cambridge University. The collection, which includes original scripts, puppet moulds, drawings and recordings, is held in the university library.

In a family statement reported by the BBC, his relatives said: “The spirit that Spitting Image brought to the country was epoch defining.” They added that Fluck “was a private man who eschewed fame and attention”, and that his creative life continued for many years after the series from his studio in Cornwall.
