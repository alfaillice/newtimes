HMS Vengeance, one of the four Trident nuclear-armed submarines operated by the Royal Navy, has returned to its base at Faslane in Scotland after a seven-month patrol that set a new record, according to The Guardian. The boat began its mission on 12 April and spent 217 days submerged, beating the previous record of 205 days set by HMS Vanguard. The defence secretary, Wes Streeting, was at Faslane to greet the crew, the newspaper reports.

Britain's nuclear deterrent relies on the principle that one of the submarines is always at sea, so the UK could still launch strikes if its land-based systems were destroyed in a nuclear war. Each Trident boat can carry up to 40 nuclear warheads. Because a submarine's location must stay secret, it cannot be resupplied during a patrol, and its crew cannot send messages home, although they can receive news.

Speaking at Faslane, Streeting said the length of time the crew had spent underwater did not sit lightly with him. He described the nuclear patrols as the "ultimate national endeavour" and praised the submariners' service and professionalism, according to The Guardian.

David Cullen, a nuclear expert at the Basic thinktank, told the newspaper: "Patrols are getting longer and longer." He argued that the fleet was designed around tours of about three months, which were already considered a long time to be submerged. Cullen said the risks of operating the submarines were increasing because of the way they are being run, and that boats nearing the end of their design life should be operated more gently.

The Guardian reports that the longer missions reflect the slow pace of submarine maintenance. Labour has blamed spending cuts made under the Conservatives, while naval chiefs insist there is a plan to improve the situation. Last week, the head of the Royal Navy said he had great respect for the Trident sailors but that submarine maintenance needed to improve to allow faster turnaround between patrols.

The pressure on crews is considerable. The Vanguard-class boats were designed to carry three to four months of food for a crew of about 140, and patrols of more than 100 days were once regarded as highly exceptional. According to the newspaper, missions now begin with tinned food stored in corridors and other spaces. Concern about the psychological effects of such long periods underwater has grown at a time when the Royal Navy is struggling to recruit.

The submarine service also faces allegations of sexual abuse and harassment during deployments, despite a rule on board banning physical intimacy. In October 2024, the then First Sea Lord, the navy's most senior officer, apologised to women serving in the submarine service for "misogyny, bullying and other unacceptable behaviours". The navy later held an inquiry after a whistleblower complaint, but its report was heavily redacted.

The replacement for the four Vanguard-class submarines, HMS Dreadnought, is not expected to be ready until about 2032, which means the existing boats are likely to remain in service for at least five more years. In July, Labour announced a £26bn plan over ten years to invest in Britain's three main naval bases, at Faslane, Devonport and Portsmouth, as part of a wider increase in defence spending, The Guardian reports.
