In September, the Conservative Party warned that British consumers and businesses would be 'dangerously exposed', with no input on how regulations are made and no power to reject them.

Speaking at a Marks & Spencer food court in central London, Falconer said the deal would lower costs for firms importing food. 'This will mean less cost for supermarkets, and I hope that less cost then turns into cheaper produce on the shelves,' he said.

Falconer said the reset could also remove checks on goods moving from Great Britain to Northern Ireland, as well as separate 'Not for EU' labelling. He put the additional value of the food part of the deal at £5bn. The whole agreement, due to be signed on Friday, is calculated to add 0.4% to UK GDP. It also includes aligning emissions trading rules and a youth mobility scheme.

Tom Bradshaw, president of the National Farmers' Union (NFU), welcomed fewer border inspections and less paperwork, but said the moment was one of 'opportunity and risk' for farmers, who are waiting to see which carve-outs and transitional arrangements are included. He said food export volumes to the EU had fallen by over a third since 2019, and urged a 'pragmatic' approach to implementing new rules. He hopes for compromise on pesticides and on precision breeding, or gene-edited crops. The EU has historically had much stricter gene-editing rules but relaxed them this year. Falconer said the details in that area would have to be seen.

Falconer also addressed concerns that stricter EU rules on certain ingredients could limit sales of some UK snack flavours, such as smoky bacon crisps. He said the UK had stayed 'pretty closely aligned' with European food safety standards since Brexit, and that Walkers and Frazzles had already changed their recipes. He said there would inevitably be trade-offs, but that the government wanted to ensure the UK's advantages were not lost.
