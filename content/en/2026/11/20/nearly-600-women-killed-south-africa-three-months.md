Nearly 600 women were killed in South Africa between July and September, according to the country's latest crime statistics, the BBC reports. The statistics were released on Friday and show 581 women killed in that period, 131 of them in Gauteng province, which includes Johannesburg. The BBC's own summary rounds this to nearly 600.

The same figures show a slight fall in murders overall. Police recorded 5,590 murders, 204 fewer than in the same period last year, which the BBC says works out at an average of about 60 a day. However, the broadcaster notes that it is not clear whether the number of women killed has gone up or down. Police had recently stopped publishing this breakdown, citing what they described as "significant anomalies" in the statistics.

The figures include 13 women whose bodies were found in public areas in and around Johannesburg. Those cases caused national outrage and put pressure on the police to find those responsible. On Thursday, the Gauteng police chief, General Tommy Mthombeni, said eight people had been arrested in connection with the 13 killings, according to the BBC. Some of the arrests relate to cases in Kempton Park, Boksburg and Tembisa, all towns in the Johannesburg area.

In a separate incident, the BBC reports that five women and a young child were shot dead in Free State province, south of Johannesburg, earlier this week. Police said three masked men opened fire on a family in the township of Botshabelo on Wednesday night, and a woman was wounded. No arrests had been made in that case, the BBC says.

South Africa's acting police minister, Firoz Cachalia, said there needed to be "greater efforts to prevent... violence" against women and children, the BBC reports. The BBC also recalls that President Cyril Ramaphosa said in September that "no stone will be left unturned" in the search for those behind the killings. Last year, Ramaphosa declared violence against women a "national disaster". The country has one of the highest rates of gender-based violence and murder in the world, the BBC notes.

All the figures in this story come from the BBC's reporting. The statistics have not been published in full in the sources consulted for this article, so the numbers should be read in that context.
