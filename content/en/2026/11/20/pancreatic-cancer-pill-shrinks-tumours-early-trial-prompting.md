According to the BBC, a daily pill being tested against pancreatic cancer appears to shrink tumours and, in some patients, to make them disappear. Experts quoted by the broadcaster have described the early results as the biggest breakthrough in treatment for the disease in decades. The drug, daraxonrasib, is being given together with chemotherapy in early-stage trials.

Pancreatic cancer is among the hardest cancers to treat and has the lowest survival rate of all common cancers in the UK, the BBC reports. Chemotherapy can reduce tumours to a degree, but the disease often grows back, and most patients with advanced cancer survive months rather than years. Cancer Research UK, the charity, estimates that around 11,500 people are diagnosed with the disease in Britain each year and around 10,200 die from it.

According to the BBC, the trial included 61 people who had recently been diagnosed with advanced pancreatic cancer that had spread to other parts of the body. Tumours stopped growing in almost all of them. Just over 60% saw their tumours shrink or disappear, and for around 8%, or one in 12 patients, the cancer became undetectable on CT scans. On average, participants were followed for about a year and a half, and some did not see their cancer return during that period.

Daraxonrasib is a KRAS inhibitor, a type of treatment that is already approved in the United States. It targets RAS mutations, which the BBC says are found in most pancreatic cancers and drive tumour growth. Dozens of other cancers, including lung and ovarian, can carry similar mutations, so the drug could in principle help other patients too.

Dr Wungki Park, of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in the United States, who is involved in the trial, told the BBC that the results were extremely encouraging so far. He pointed to a higher proportion of patients responding, deeper responses and a near doubling in the time the disease was kept under control, which he said might eventually lead to longer survival. Prof Elena Garralda, chair of the European cancer conference EORTC, where the work is being presented to other doctors, said the combined treatment could significantly improve overall survival as well as the time before tumours start to grow again.

Cancer Research UK called the results “the biggest leap forward in decades”. Its Dr Sam Godfrey said the drug had found “a way of drugging the undruggable”, but he also cautioned that a patient can only be considered cancer-free after more time has passed, even when scans show no sign of the disease.

Doctors stress that the work is still at an early stage. The BBC notes that longer studies are needed to establish whether these cancers can go into remission and for how long, and that the treatment should not be seen as a cure. What the trial offers, in the words of the report, is the hope that patients may gain more time.
