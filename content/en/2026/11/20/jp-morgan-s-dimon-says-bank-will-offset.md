Jamie Dimon, chief executive of JP Morgan, has said the bank will look for ways to offset an increase in the UK tax on banks that he describes as unfair, according to The Guardian. Speaking at an event in London on Thursday, Dimon said he would seek to claw the money back and that he was "setting an example so that [governments] understand the trade-offs of these unfair penalties".

The measure at the centre of the dispute is the bank surcharge, an extra levy on banks' UK balance sheets. The Guardian reports that it will rise from 3 per cent to 6 per cent in January and that the increase is temporary, lasting 15 months. The chancellor, the UK's finance minister, John Healey, announced the change in his first budget in October.

The Guardian says it is unclear how JP Morgan, which employs 23,000 people in the UK, would make up for the extra cost. In comments first reported by the Financial Times, citing people who attended the event, Dimon said the decision was taken with sorrow rather than anger.

Dimon has lobbied the Labour government repeatedly against raising taxes on banks. In May he suggested he could scrap plans for a new £3bn tower in the Canary Wharf district of London, which was expected to serve as the bank's UK headquarters. After the October budget, a source familiar with the matter told the Guardian that work on the 279,000 sq metre (3m sq ft) building was still going ahead. The bank said at the time that the policy lacked principle because it singled out the sector, and that it operated in a highly competitive global market.

JP Morgan also runs a large campus in Bournemouth, where more than 5,300 staff process more than $1tn (£750bn) of global transactions each day, according to the Guardian. The newspaper reported earlier this month that staff there feared the spread of artificial intelligence could put their jobs at risk.

The Guardian explains that UK lenders already pay a corporation tax rate of 28 per cent, above the standard 25 per cent, and the separate surcharge. The FT, as cited by the Guardian, reported that JP Morgan, the largest bank in the US, expected to pay £120m more in taxes because of the increase. Bank shares rallied after the October announcement because the rise was smaller than many in the industry had expected.
