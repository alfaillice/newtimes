President Donald Trump said on Friday that US technology companies building artificial intelligence (AI) data centres outside the United States would face "tremendous penalties". The BBC reported the same threat as a "tremendous tax". Trump gave no details of how such a policy would work, and said he had not yet told his own officials about it.

Data centres are large warehouse-style buildings that house the advanced computer chips and servers needed to run AI programmes such as ChatGPT and Claude. Speaking at a White House press briefing, Trump said American companies should keep this infrastructure at home. "We're going to keep it all in this country," he said, according to the BBC. He argued that China would otherwise take all of the facilities.

The remarks came days after a change in US policy on advanced chips. On Monday, Washington said companies would need a licence to buy the most advanced chips, so that officials can check whether they are being used by Chinese firms or smuggled into China. The Guardian described separate new rules restricting remote access to US chips and servers, which the administration said were designed to limit China's ability to train its AI models. The BBC said the licensing move would affect South East Asian countries that have invested heavily in data centres.

Big tech companies have already looked abroad. The BBC reported that Meta and Google have agreements to build data centres overseas. In September, Google announced a €13bn ($15bn; £11bn) investment in AI infrastructure in Finland, including funding for three data centres. Building work on two of those sites was halted last month over concerns about clearing more than 300 hectares of forest. Trump singled out the Finnish project, and Canada has positioned itself as an alternative location, with Meta breaking ground in Alberta and Anthropic signing a long-term agreement to lease a proposed site in Nova Scotia.

Data centres were also a major issue in the midterm elections on 3 November. According to the Pew Research Centre, cited by the BBC, the US had about 3,000 data centres as of April, with another 1,500 in various stages of approval or construction. Many are in rural areas, and concerns about noise and energy use helped Democratic candidates who promised tougher rules win. Trump, by contrast, urged communities to extract payments from developers rather than shut them out.

The BBC said it had contacted Meta for comment. Al Jazeera said it had contacted the White House but had not received a reply. It remains unclear how a penalty regime would be enforced, or whether it would be introduced at all.
