Hungarian authorities have found nearly $20m in cash hidden across the country during an investigation into a former minister of Viktor Orbán, according to the BBC. The broadcaster reported that the money was found in safes, buried in woodland and concealed in agricultural, residential and storage buildings in secluded locations, citing a statement from the National Asset Recovery and Protection Office (NVVH).

The former minister at the centre of the case is Miklós Seszták, who served as minister of national development for four of the 16 years Orbán governed Hungary, the BBC said. Seszták is accused of accepting bribes as part of a scheme involving the state transport company. He has denied any wrongdoing, the broadcaster added.

Orbán was unseated as prime minister in a landslide election in April by Péter Magyar, who had promised a sweeping anti-corruption drive. The BBC reported that after a two-day search, Magyar said on Friday that the discovery marked the end of the "mafia". The NVVH was set up to investigate alleged corruption during Orbán's time in office, and Magyar has called the wider effort "Operation Purgatory".

The NVVH listed the seized cash as 5,071,632,000 forints, €2,658,350 and $1,257,100, which it valued at $19.5m combined. According to the BBC, the money has been deposited in the Hungarian State Treasury. The broadcaster gives the total as nearly $20m (£14.8m).

The BBC also reported that Seszták and another former minister, along with the former central bank chief György Matolcsy, have been under arrest since September in separate graft investigations. All three deny wrongdoing. Allegations of corruption and cronyism dogged Orbán's government, which the EU characterised as an "electoral autocracy". The government faced accusations of offering lucrative public projects to allies, claims Orbán has strongly denied.

In a separate case, the BBC reported that Hungarian authorities detained Orbán's brother and three others on Monday as part of a people-smuggling investigation. The former prime minister dismissed the action as political revenge, according to the broadcaster, and said the aim was to eliminate political opponents and establish a one-party system.

The BBC's account is based on a single outlet, and the figures are those published by the NVVH as relayed by the broadcaster.
