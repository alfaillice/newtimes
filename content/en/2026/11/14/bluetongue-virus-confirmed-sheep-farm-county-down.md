Bluetongue, a viral disease that affects ruminant animals, has been confirmed on a farm in Kilkeel, County Down, according to the BBC. The broadcaster reports that officials from the Department of Agriculture, Environment and Rural Affairs (DAERA), the government department responsible for farming and the environment in Northern Ireland, detected the virus in three sheep. The BBC describes the case as the first of Bluetongue in Northern Ireland this season.

The BBC reports that no additional movement restrictions or disease control zones will be introduced. The reason, according to the broadcaster, is that the virus has been present in Northern Ireland since November 2025. The disease was first detected near Bangor, also in County Down, that month, and a number of other herds along the eastern coast of Northern Ireland have since been affected. The BBC's account thus describes Kilkeel as the first case of the season while also saying the virus has been in the region for almost a year, a point the report does not reconcile in detail.

The minister, Andrew Muir, described the discovery as "disappointing but not unexpected", given how widely the disease has spread in Great Britain and Ireland, the BBC reports. The BBC notes that the Republic of Ireland has recorded more than a hundred cases in recent months, and that cases in England have been rising, with 2,950 now confirmed. The broadcaster also reports that a mild winter means more of the biting midge that spreads the disease are likely to have survived and become infectious this season.

The Chief Veterinary Officer, Brian Dooher, urged farmers to watch their livestock for any symptoms, the BBC says. Dooher said suspected cases on farms should be reported to DAERA. According to the report, active surveillance is under way at abattoirs, and susceptible animals imported from mainland EU countries are being tested after arrival. He also stressed the importance of sourcing animals safely and responsibly.

Muir stressed that Bluetongue poses no threat to public health or food safety, and that milk and meat are safe to consume, as the BBC quotes him. The disease affects cattle, sheep, goats and deer. Muir urged all farmers to continue strict biosecurity measures and to report any suspicion of disease to DAERA.

All the information in this story comes from a single outlet, the BBC, citing DAERA and its officials.
