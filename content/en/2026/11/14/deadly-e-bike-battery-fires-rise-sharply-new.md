Fires caused by lithium-ion batteries in e-bikes have become increasingly frequent in New York City, causing deaths and growing anxiety among residents and officials, according to The Guardian. The newspaper reports that the New York City Fire Department (FDNY) has recorded more than 400 such fires so far this year, with 83 people injured and 12 killed. In 2025 there were 292 fires, 51 injuries and one death.

The Guardian describes two recent incidents. In late October, four members of one family died in a fire in an 11th-floor apartment in a public housing building in the Bronx. The blaze started from a battery in an e-bike that was blocking a route to an exit. Later, a fire at a home in Brooklyn killed six people and injured nine, including three firefighters and two police officers. Investigators found lithium-ion batteries at the scene but have not established whether they caused the fire, the paper said, citing the city council speaker, Julie Menin. In a statement, Menin said more must be done to encourage and require safe storage practices. "Enough is enough," she said.

Lithium-ion batteries power e-bikes and scooters, and overcharging or damaged batteries are often blamed for fires. The Guardian notes that London and Toronto have also seen sharp rises in such fires. Part of the increase in New York reflects the growing popularity of e-bikes, which are widely used by food delivery workers and commuters. Citing the Light Electric Vehicle Association, the paper says 2.2 million e-bikes were imported into the United States in 2025, double the number three years earlier.

The city's main response has been to move charging out of homes. According to the Guardian, a 2024 pilot run by the New York City Department of Transportation used battery-swapping cabinets, where delivery workers could exchange flat batteries for charged ones. Riders made more than 12,000 swaps over six months, and the city said participants cut home charging by 35 per cent. The city then announced 25 charging sites, but the paper reports that the timetable has shifted. One announcement said installation would not begin until 2027, and the transport department now says it remains on track to launch the service in 2028.

The Guardian also reports that a 2023 federal grant of $25m was meant to fund 173 charging and storage stations at 53 public housing developments. The then mayor, Eric Adams, said residents would no longer need to charge their e-bikes in their flats. The newspaper says the federal government has not released the money, according to the transport department.

Popwheels, a company involved in the pilot, has installed about 45 cabinets across the city, according to its chief executive, Baruch Herzfeld. He wants to install hundreds more but says the permitting process is slow, often taking six months to approve a single cabinet. He also said the city has issued tickets to property owners hosting the cabinets for minor issues, such as a fire extinguisher with incorrect tags. "This permitting process is why kids are dying," he said.

The FDNY rejected that account. Its deputy commissioner for public information, Amanda Farinacci, told the paper that the tickets addressed significant concerns, not minor infractions. She added that the department works closely with the industry and that linking its regulatory role to people charging batteries indoors was misplaced.

Laura Kavanagh, a former FDNY commissioner who now works at New York University, told the Guardian that the department was focusing on too narrow a concern. She argued that a battery failure in an outdoor cabinet would most likely cause only property damage, and that this risk should be weighed against the deaths of 10 New Yorkers in fires over the past three weeks.

The city council will hold an emergency meeting on 20 November, Menin announced on Friday. It will examine whether existing laws are being enforced and how the public is being taught fire safety and lithium-ion battery storage. Mayor Zohran Mamdani told MS Now, after the Brooklyn fire, that e-bike fires can cost lives and livelihoods, and that the city is considering every option to keep people safe.
