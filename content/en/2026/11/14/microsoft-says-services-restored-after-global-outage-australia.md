Microsoft has said its services have been fully restored after a worldwide outage that disrupted its cloud-based software for roughly a day. In a statement early on Saturday, the company said work on the underlying problem had been completed and that customers using Outlook, Teams and Copilot should no longer be affected. The Guardian, however, reported that Microsoft was still bringing services back gradually to keep them stable.

Users began reporting trouble on Thursday. The BBC said the disruption affected more than 70 countries and that more than 430 million people use the affected products. Among the organisations hit were the Japanese government, the Philippines Supreme Court, Australia's domestic spy agency, parts of the UK's National Health Service (NHS), and staff at the US House of Representatives. Microsoft first acknowledged that users in the Americas could not reach Exchange Online mailboxes at 18.54 CET on Thursday. By 05.36 CET on Friday it was reporting only a partial recovery in some regions. By 14.32 CET it described broader improvements in North America and sustained improvements across much of Europe.

In Australia, the Guardian reported that experts warned costs could run into billions of dollars and that some small businesses could be forced to close. Ashwin Pal, a partner at the risk advisory firm RSM Australia, said his clients had activated continuity plans, but many businesses had no fallback. The Australian Computer Society called on the federal government to review which critical IT systems depend on a single provider. Some hospitals and clinics were affected. The Australian Medical Association said some facilities had returned to paper prescriptions, although it reported no impact on patient care. Service NSW asked customers to delay visits to its service centres, and courts in South Australia and Victoria reported email problems. The head of ASIO, Australia's domestic spy agency, Mike Burgess, said its systems had been affected "on one level, yes, but not completely".

The two outlets gave different accounts of the outage's length. The BBC said services took nearly 24 hours to begin recovering, while the Guardian put the period at roughly 18 hours. The sources also describe the cause differently. Microsoft told the BBC the problem was introduced during a "network capacity upgrade" and was not a cyber-attack or the work of an artificial intelligence agent. The Guardian reported that Microsoft had pointed to problems with settings applied to servers that keep Microsoft 365 running. Pal argued that a change should have been tested before it was released.

Microsoft apologised. A spokesperson said the company was "deeply sorry" for the disruption. The company has promised a full post-incident report once its review is complete.

Experts quoted by the BBC said the outage showed how much organisations depend on a small group of large technology providers. Matt Saunders, field chief technology officer at the consultancy Adaptavist, said it would be hard to protect against a similar failure, because it was almost impossible for a business to work without tools such as Microsoft's.
