Probation officers across England and Wales have voted to take industrial action over pay and workloads, a move that The Guardian says complicates the government's plan to release thousands of prisoners early to relieve overcrowding in jails.

According to The Guardian, members of Napo, the union that represents probation staff and workers in family courts, backed overtime restrictions and other forms of action short of a strike. A full strike is likely to start on 23 November, the paper reports, and plans are being made for further strike action at a later stage.

The union says it called the ballot because of what it describes as an “inadequate” 5 per cent pay offer from ministers and because workloads have not been reduced. Napo had sought a 12 per cent increase. Its general secretary, Ian Lawrence, told The Guardian that the result reflected serious anger at the state of probation workloads, which were already in crisis before the latest early release scheme.

The ballot results, as reported by The Guardian, were as follows. On pay, 85 per cent of members voted in favour of action short of a strike and 74 per cent in favour of strike action. On workloads, the figures were 87 per cent and 75 per cent respectively. Napo also says probation wages have stagnated since 2010, and that reception staff earn a salary just above the minimum wage, the lowest in the civil service.

## Pressure on probation services

Probation officers monitor offenders' behaviour, oversee their rehabilitation, track electronic tags and can return people to prison for offences or breaches of their licence, such as entering a zone from which they are excluded. The Guardian reports that the latest early release scheme, launched in October to ease pressure on prisons, will see about 2,500 offenders released by the end of the year, including sex offenders, domestic abusers and stalkers.

The Institute for Government, a thinktank, told The Guardian that the service has faced a serious staff shortage for several years. It said the number of probation officers has consistently been 25 to 30 per cent below target staffing levels. As of March 2026, nine probation delivery units, which make up 8 per cent of the total, had a vacancy rate of 40 per cent or more. The thinktank said slow progress on recruitment means practitioners remain overstretched.

## Government response

A spokesperson for the Ministry of Justice (MoJ), the department responsible for prisons and probation in England and Wales, told The Guardian that the offer includes a further 5 per cent pay rise for every worker. The spokesperson said this follows the largest probation pay increase in more than a decade, alongside record investment. The MoJ is also recruiting 1,300 additional trainee probation officers for the 2026-27 financial year, as part of a £700m package running to 2028-29.

The spokesperson said the department was disappointed that some staff had voted against the offer. The MoJ added that it has “robust contingency plans” to ensure offenders remain under the same level of supervision. The Guardian's report does not say how those plans would work in practice.
