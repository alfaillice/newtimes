According to The Guardian, the Fan Bay plotting room, a concrete command bunker about four metres deep in the white cliffs of Dover, will be open to visitors from Friday on National Trust guided tours. The site was kept secret for decades and is reached through a hatch in a field.

The newspaper reports that the bunker was ordered by Winston Churchill and housed what it describes as the first land-based computer-aided targeting system. It acted as the "brain" for a gun battery that defended Dover and attacked German ships in the Channel, according to Jon Stewart, who led the excavation for the National Trust.

Stewart was the first person to enter the bunker when it was rediscovered in 2017, the Guardian says. It had been quietly mothballed in 1957 and classified until 1981 because of its military importance. Describing the moment, Stewart told the paper it was a "Tutankhamun moment" for a conflict archaeologist.

The Guardian links the plotting room and its artillery directly to Churchill. Soon after his "fight them on the beaches" speech, he visited the Kent coast and was alarmed to see enemy ships passing through the Strait of Dover unchallenged. Dover was also on the front line of bombardment from enemy guns in occupied France. In a July 1940 memo, Churchill wrote that Britain had to win control of the straits with artillery. According to the paper, the guns were ready within 100 days and the bunker was completed 64 days later. Stewart said the guns were known locally as "Churchill's pets".

The plotting room housed a 1.8-metre electromechanical computer known as a fire direction table. The Guardian reports that it calculated the position of moving ships when shells landed and sent firing instructions to gun crews. Up to 10 people at a time worked there. Stewart pointed to rough openings cut through walls and floors for cabling, which the paper says show the equipment was installed before the layout drawings were available. The computer was deliberately destroyed in 1957 to keep it out of enemy hands.

Only a handful of the operators are known. They include the actor Wallas Eaton, who later appeared as Senator Ludicrus Sextus in the television comedy Up Pompeii alongside Frankie Howerd, and a lance bombardier named WP Bryant, recorded as a plotter. Stewart told the paper he would like to hear from families of people who served at Fan Bay, because the records are scant.

The site has been preserved as the team found it. Remnants include old newspapers on the floor, old shoes and official notices. One service tunnel has chalked graffiti reading "heroes fan hole Dodds and Petitt". Stewart told the Guardian that the two men may have been responsible for laying the cables.

Stewart compared the discovery to the rescue of Bletchley Park, the Buckinghamshire codebreaking site, in the 1990s. He described the plotting room as "a new hidden history". In the paper's account, he said the team chose to conserve the room rather than recreate it, so that visitors can see the marks of its history for themselves.
