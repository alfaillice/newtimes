An international court has rejected Argentina's request to suspend the Sea Lion oil project off the Falkland Islands, ruling that the dispute over sovereignty lies outside its jurisdiction. The decision by the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (Itlos) was announced on Saturday 28 November and is a significant setback for the government of President Javier Milei.

The Falklands are a British overseas territory in the South Atlantic with roughly 3,500 inhabitants. Argentina calls them Las Malvinas and has never abandoned its claim to them. The two countries fought a 74-day war over the islands in 1982, after Argentine forces landed there and a British task force pushed them out. Successive Argentine governments have pressed the claim with varying degrees of force and diplomacy. The UK government says sovereignty rests with Britain and that the islanders have the right to self-determination. In a 2013 referendum, islanders voted overwhelmingly to remain a British territory.

Itlos president Maurice Kamga read out the decision. The tribunal said there did not appear to be any basis for believing it had jurisdiction, because it could not rule on questions of sovereignty. Kamga said 18 judges had voted to reject Argentina's claims and three had voted to uphold them. At a hearing earlier this month, Argentina had argued that drilling should not go ahead until sovereignty was settled, and asked the tribunal for a temporary injunction while a full arbitration could take place.

The Sea Lion oilfield lies about 130 miles (209 km) from the islands, in the North Falkland basin. It is led by Israel's Navitas Petroleum and its British partner, Rockhopper Exploration. According to the sources, drilling is due to start in March, with extraction from 2028 continuing for 35 years. The field is estimated to contain 1.7 billion barrels of oil. The islands' government sees the project as a way to secure a self-financed future, and it is expected to double the territory's GDP, with a cumulative uplift of £8bn over its lifetime.

UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband and Attorney General for England and Wales Ellie Reeves said in a joint statement: “Argentina sought to stop the Falkland Islanders making their own sovereign decisions and failed in this attempt.” They also called on Argentina to respect the islanders' right to determine their own future and to end what they described as its wider economic pressure on the territory. During the hearing, Reeves had said that an order halting the project would have “devastating consequences” for the islanders.

Argentina's position is different. A spokesperson for Milei said the tribunal's ruling was not the “final word” on the dispute, and that the government would keep taking action against what it called the unilateral exploitation of its resources. The spokesperson said new sanction proceedings had been opened against five entities. Milei has previously described the companies' activities as unlawful and has imposed economic sanctions on people and firms involved in exploration around the islands. Its government is also pushing through legislation that would criminalise parties involved in the Falklands economy. During the hearing, Mario Oyarzábal, the chief legal adviser to Argentina's foreign ministry, argued that oil revenues would finance the military presence that sustains British control of the territory.

The arbitration tribunal will now address the substance of the dispute, but its ruling could take years. By then, oil production is likely to have already begun. Navitas welcomed the decision to reject all of Argentina's requests for provisional measures and said it would continue developing the project under licences issued by the Falkland Islands government.
