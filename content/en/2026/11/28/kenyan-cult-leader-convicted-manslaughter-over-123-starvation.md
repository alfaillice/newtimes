A court in Mombasa, on Kenya's Indian Ocean coast, has convicted Paul Mackenzie, the self-styled pastor who founded the Good News International Church, of manslaughter over the deaths of 123 of his followers. The verdict was delivered on Friday. Twenty-nine other people were also found guilty, including Mackenzie's wife, Rhoda Mumbua Maweu. Sixty defendants were acquitted, and the Director of Public Prosecutions, the office that leads state prosecutions in Kenya, has said it will appeal against those acquittals.

Mackenzie set up the church in 2003 and built a following of thousands across branches in Kenya. A television station broadcast his sermons from his church in the resort town of Malindi. He preached that education was evil, that healthcare was against the doctrine and that digital identity cards were the mark of the devil. After the main church closed in 2019, he moved his followers to the Shakahola forest, where they lived in mud houses spread across the area.

Police arrested him in 2023 after a tip-off. Investigators then found more than 400 bodies in shallow graves in the forest. Many of the dead had starved, and in some cases the remains were too skeletal to establish a cause of death.

In his verdict, the magistrate, Alex Ithuku, said Mackenzie "gave the directions to fast to death". The prosecution argued that Mackenzie and his accomplices had agreed a suicide pact to fast until they died in order to meet Jesus. According to the Director of Public Prosecutions' statement, the cult planned the deaths in phases: children first, then women, then men, with Mackenzie meant to die last. The defendants argued that the victims had chosen to fast of their own free will. Mackenzie had pleaded not guilty and one of his lawyers said he would appeal after sentencing.

This is not the first verdict against him. On 6 November he was sentenced to 200 years in prison for the murder of 191 people, 190 of them children. This week he was also convicted of child cruelty, assault, radicalisation and terrorism-related offences. He is due to be sentenced for the child cruelty and radicalisation charges in December. The prosecutors describe Friday's conviction as the final one in the cases against him.

The deaths have raised questions about how such a group could operate for so long without official intervention. In 2023 President William Ruto said he took responsibility, as head of state, for the government's failure to prevent the deaths. He set up a commission of inquiry, but a court later declared it unconstitutional. The killings have also led to calls for tighter oversight of religious groups.

Mass deaths linked to cults have occurred elsewhere. In 2000 a doomsday group in Uganda killed more than 700 people, and in 1978 more than 900 followers of an American cult leader died in Guyana after drinking poison.
