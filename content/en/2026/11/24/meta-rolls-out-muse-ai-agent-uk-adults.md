Meta has begun rolling out Muse, its personal AI agent, to adults in the UK. The company is promoting the tool as a way to hand over everyday chores, from chasing refunds and renewing car insurance to doing the Christmas shopping. The launch was described in a blog post published on Tuesday, and it is the first product of this kind from a major technology company to reach UK users.

An AI agent is a program that carries out a series of tasks without a person directing each step. Muse can be used as a standalone app or inside WhatsApp, the messaging service that Meta owns. Users are invited to give the assistant a name, a appearance and a personality, then ask it to send emails, book travel, buy presents or look for a cheaper energy deal. Meta also suggests it can organise a school calendar.

The product has already been popular in the United States, where it reached the top of the free app charts on Google and Apple's stores and has been downloaded millions of times. Meta says Muse cannot make a purchase or send an email without the user's permission, and that it only connects to the apps a user chooses to link. The company says the app cannot see passwords or payment details and does not share conversations with advertisers. Before buying something or sending a message, Muse asks for approval. A separate “security agent” checks its actions against the permissions the user has granted.

Users can decide whether their interactions help train Meta's AI, but they must actively switch that setting off. In its blog post, Meta described Muse as “private, safe and secure by design”. The company has also signed agreements with British businesses, including Marks & Spencer, the electrical retailer Currys, Sainsbury's and easyJet, to make it easier for Muse to navigate their websites.

The launch comes under scrutiny from British regulators. The Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum, which brings together the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), the communications regulator Ofcom and the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), published a statement last month. It said AI agents could bring new innovation but could also “amplify and accelerate risks”, including fraud, scams and misuse of personal information. Meta says it has engaged constructively with the relevant authorities and will continue to do so.

Earlier reports said that Meta staff had raised concerns internally about actions taken by the agent during its US debut. Examples included a reminder to reply to a message from a relative on Signal, an encrypted messaging app, and a message that contained random bank account balances. Meta has described these problems as isolated. It is understood that most of the issues flagged related to a bug that has since been fixed. The company says internal testing is essential to make safety and privacy protections work before a product is released to the public.

Muse runs on Muse Spark, the first model from the company's “super intelligence” AI lab. On Sunday, Meta's chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, wrote on social media that humanity could lose control over AI systems able to improve themselves without human involvement. He also said the industry needed to make rapid progress on AI safety, security and alignment, meaning the effort to ensure AI systems follow human goals and values.
