Heat-related deaths in England reached a record high this summer, according to the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA). It estimates that 5,258 deaths were associated with extreme heat between May and September 2026. The provisional figure is the highest annual total since records began in 2016, and it is far above the previous peak of 2,985, recorded in 2022.

The UKHSA stresses that the figures are estimates of deaths associated with heat, not a count of deaths directly caused by it. They are also provisional, because death registration data for the period is not yet complete. Earlier annual totals were 2,295 in 2023, 1,311 in 2024 and 1,504 in 2025.

The summer was the hottest on record in the UK, according to provisional Met Office data. Temperatures reached 38C in Norfolk in June and 38.2C in Gloucestershire in August. England also had its driest July on record, with just 7mm of rain.

The deadliest stretch was between 21 and 28 June, when an estimated 2,401 people died. More than 3,000 heat-associated deaths were recorded across the first two heat episodes of the season, in May and June. The agency counted seven heat episodes lasting 41 days in total, the highest number of episodes and heat-episode days since monitoring began in 2016. The June heatwave triggered only the second red heat-health alert ever issued in England.

The UKHSA said heat early in the summer can be particularly dangerous, because people and public services may not yet be prepared. It said heat-health measures should be in place from the start of summer, not only during the hottest weeks. Dr Ross Thompson, the agency's principal environmental health scientist, said climate change is already affecting people's health in England and that extreme heat is expected to become longer, more frequent and more intense.

Health Secretary Yvette Cooper, speaking in the House of Commons, the lower chamber of Parliament, said she wanted to offer condolences to everyone who lost a loved one this summer. "The numbers are a stark reminder of the human impact of climate change," she said. She added that she had asked the NHS to prepare for summer as seriously as it does for winter.

Campaigners also responded. Maja Darlington, a climate campaigner at Greenpeace, argued that deaths on this scale reflect policy choices. She called on the government to heat-proof schools, hospitals and care homes, and criticised decisions on the Rosebank oil field, electric vehicle targets and Heathrow expansion. Emma Howard Boyd, chair of the National Heat Risk Commission and a professor at the Grantham Research Institute on Climate Change and the Environment, called the figures a stark warning. She pointed to a Climate Change Committee projection of up to 10,000 heat-associated deaths a year across the UK by 2050.

The UKHSA said that adapting homes, neighbourhoods, and health and care systems to higher temperatures will become increasingly important, alongside cutting emissions to limit future warming.
