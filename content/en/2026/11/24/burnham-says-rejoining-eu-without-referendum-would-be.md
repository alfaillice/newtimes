Andy Burnham has said it would be ‘unthinkable’ for the United Kingdom to rejoin the European Union without holding another referendum. It is the firmest commitment he has yet made to asking voters directly if he concludes that Britain should go back into the bloc.

The remark came on Monday in the House of Commons, the lower house of the UK Parliament. Asked whether he could give a clear statement that he would not take the country back in without a new public vote, Burnham, the Labour prime minister, replied: ‘Yes, it would be unthinkable.’ Reports of the exchange note that it is the first time he has ruled out a return without a special vote on the matter.

Britain voted to leave the EU in a referendum on 23 June 2016, by 52 per cent to 48 per cent. The result led to the resignation of the then prime minister, David Cameron, and began more than four years of negotiations over the terms of departure.

Burnham made his comments days after a summit in Brussels. The deal he signed there on Friday covers food and drink checks, emissions trading and youth mobility, and he has described it as a landmark that will lower the cost of living. He will introduce legislation next week to implement the food and drink part.

He has also hit back at former Conservative prime ministers Rishi Sunak, David Cameron and Theresa May, who have urged him not to reopen the Brexit debate. Burnham said they had left Britain with a bad deal and were ‘stuck in the past’. He told MPs, however, that his government would take a ‘pragmatic approach’ to the future relationship and that there was no fixed destination inside Downing Street.

According to reporting on the government’s plans, Burnham is expected to set out long-term options for closer ties next month, with concrete steps coming after the next general election. Downing Street insiders expect that, if he concludes that rejoining is the best option, he would seek a mandate in the next Labour manifesto to start negotiations and then hold a confirmatory referendum.

The opposition was quick to respond. Kemi Badenoch, leader of the Conservative Party, accused him of having ‘changed his tune’ after winning a by-election in Makerfield on a promise not to reopen the Brexit debate. She also said he could not unite his own Cabinet. Nigel Farage, leader of the right-wing Reform UK party, said the issue ‘may well be’ what revives his party, which lost its lead in the polls to Labour over the summer.

The two sides have agreed to a ‘brainstorming’ process on closer ties, which covers options such as a Swiss-style deal, a single market and customs union arrangement, and a full return to the EU. Officials expect the results to be presented around the next UK-EU summit, which is unlikely to take place before next summer. Burnham has said the discussions will run alongside a national debate with the public.

The legal text of the Brussels deal is expected in the coming days. It is likely to include a clause that would make the UK pay the costs of reinstating customs infrastructure if it reneged on the agreement. Opposition parties have called it the ‘Farage clause’, because it would apply if Farage won the next election and tried to reverse the deal.
