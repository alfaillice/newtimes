Negotiators at the UN climate conference in Antalya, Türkiye, closed the talks on Sunday without a final agreement. The European Union and a number of other countries rejected the proposed deal because it did not mention fossil fuels, the main cause of global warming. The talks were due to end on Friday but ran into Sunday, making them the longest UN climate conference in history.

About 200 countries spent two weeks negotiating under the joint presidencies of Türkiye and Australia. The text at the centre of the dispute, known as the “Antalya pact”, was drafted by those two presidencies. It invites countries to electrify their economies, asks rich nations to provide finance to help poorer ones adapt to climate impacts, and calls on countries to limit the “overshoot” of the 1.5C warming threshold set under the 2015 Paris Agreement.

The draft did not endorse the “transition away from fossil fuels” that more than 130 countries had demanded. It included provisions on carbon capture and storage (CCS), a technology that some critics say prolongs the use of fossil fuels. A new draft published early on Sunday did not contain the words “fossil fuels” at all. Its only indirect reference was to a 2023 agreement at COP28 to move away from them in energy systems.

Ireland’s minister Darragh O’Brien, speaking for the EU, said the bloc would not accept the text. “It is unconscionable that we would have a climate pact that makes no mention of fossil fuels,” he said. Panama, the Marshall Islands and Norway raised similar objections, and Pacific island nations and other small island states also opposed the draft. Saudi Arabia defended the inclusion of CCS. The African Group, China and Pakistan did not block the text, although some had reservations.

Finance was another point of contention. Poorer countries said the draft contained no clear financial commitments from richer nations to help them adapt. At COP30 in Brazil last year, countries agreed to triple adaptation finance, but the Antalya text gave no concrete figures and no baseline against which that pledge would be measured. Critics said this could give rich nations too much room to manoeuvre.

Murat Kurum, Türkiye’s COP31 president, acknowledged that there was no consensus on the draft “cover decision”, which therefore was not adopted. He said the presidency had not imposed the text or issued a “take it or leave it” ultimatum. Several other decisions were passed, including on a “just transition” mechanism to support workers and communities moving to clean energy. Australia’s climate minister Chris Bowen, the other co-president, referred to “unbridgeable red lines” in the negotiations.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres, attending his last COP before he steps down, said he was “deeply disappointed”. He blamed “powerful fossil fuel interests” for blocking action. Simon Stiell, the UN’s climate chief, said the consensus-based process “fell short today”, but that it had worked before. He said he would seek advice on possible reforms. The next conference, COP32, is scheduled for November 2027 in Ethiopia.

The collapse of a COP’s main political agreement on this scale last happened in Copenhagen in 2009. The UN says current government policies would still allow warming of about 2.8C by 2100, and the world is likely to exceed 1.5C of long-term warming in the next few years. Countries have committed to eventually bringing warming back down to that level, but the technologies needed to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere at scale do not yet exist.
