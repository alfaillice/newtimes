M John Harrison has become the oldest winner of the Booker Prize, according to the BBC, after being named the winner of the £50,000 award for *The End of Everything*, a post-apocalyptic novel. The announcement was made at a ceremony in London on Monday.

The BBC reports that Harrison, who is 81, has built a cult following over a career of around 60 years. He began writing in the 1960s and has published 13 novels, five short story collections and an "anti-memoir", the broadcaster says. It adds that he has surpassed Margaret Atwood as the oldest ever Booker winner. The BBC also reports that Booker Prize chief executive Gaby Wood described him as the first winner to come from the world of science fiction, and called him "a genuinely original thinker".

The Booker Prize is an annual award for the best novel written in English and published in the year up to 30 September. It was launched in 1969.

According to the BBC, the novel is set in the years after a mysterious catastrophe that caused British society to collapse. It follows a man and his aunt as they try to get by on the south-east coast of England, after an unexplained disaster and the arrival of mysterious alien beings. Harrison has said he deliberately left out as much background explanation as he could, so that readers would share the disorientation of the characters.

The BBC notes that the book has divided opinion. It reports that The Guardian praised the novel but said it would not suit every reader. The New York Times said the more readers learn about the conditions of the broken world, the harder it is to grasp. The Literary Review called it a work of "uncanny beauty", and the Telegraph said reading it felt more like dreaming.

The chair of the judges, Dame Mary Beard, described the book as challenging but said some of the best fiction is challenging. She also said it leaves readers with a sense of optimism, despite its subject matter. The BBC reports that the panel, which also included Patricia Lockwood, Jarvis Cocker, Raymond Antrobus and Rebecca Liu, spent a full day debating the six shortlisted novels before reaching a unanimous decision.

Speaking to BBC Radio 4's Today programme on Tuesday, Harrison said he had wanted to satirise the constant flow of information people face without the tools to analyse it. In his words, the novel builds a disaster in which the failure is one of understanding.
