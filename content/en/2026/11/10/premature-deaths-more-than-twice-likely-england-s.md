People living in the most deprived areas of England are more than twice as likely to die early as those in the most affluent areas, according to new analysis reported by The Guardian. The research, carried out by the Health Foundation, found a rate of 815 premature deaths per 100,000 people in the most deprived areas, compared with 353 per 100,000 in the least deprived. That makes the risk 2.3 times higher.

The analysis defined premature death as any adult dying before their 75th birthday. It combined demographic data from the 2021 census with records of deaths from March 2021 to December 2023, the newspaper said.

Employment and qualifications also made a difference. According to The Guardian, women with no qualifications had a premature mortality rate 2.5 times higher than women with a degree or higher. Men who were long-term unemployed or had never worked had a rate 3.5 times that of men in higher managerial, administrative and professional occupations.

Regional gaps were widest in the north-east, the north-west and Yorkshire and The Humber, and narrowest in London. For women who had never worked or were long-term unemployed, the rate in the north-west was 796 deaths per 100,000, which The Guardian put at 72 per cent higher than London's rate of 462.

The Guardian also reported that the research points to a decline in healthy life expectancy. It cited earlier analysis suggesting the UK population's health is poor and getting worse, and that it is not improving steadily as it has in countries such as Japan, Norway and Spain.

The ethnic breakdown was mixed. Black Caribbean men had the highest male rate of premature death, at 573 per 100,000, closely followed by white British men at 537. Chinese men had the lowest, at 284. Among women, white British and Black Caribbean women had the highest rates, at 370 and 367 respectively, and Chinese women the lowest, at 181.

Jabeer Butt, chief executive of the Race Equality Foundation, told The Guardian that lower premature mortality in some ethnic groups does not mean those groups have better health, or that racism is unimportant. He said the analysis showed the need to look beyond ethnicity alone, at how migration, poverty, employment, housing and discrimination interact.

Freya Tracey, principal data analyst at the Health Foundation, said: "These findings lay bare the scale of the inequalities that continue to shape people's chances of living a long and healthy life." She called for a cross-government approach covering education, employment and local economic conditions, and said the government's devolution plans should support local prevention work.

The figures describe associations between deprivation and early death. The Guardian's report does not say, on the basis of this analysis alone, why those gaps exist. The newspaper said the Department of Health and Social Care had been approached for comment.
